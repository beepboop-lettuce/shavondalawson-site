Who Protects the People Who Protect Our Children?

November 2, 2026

An honest conversation about childcare leadership, accountability, and the voices our industry continues to overlook.

By Shavonda Lawson | Founder, Educator & Childcare Advocate

I’ve been the teacher.

I’ve been the assistant.

I’ve been the school cook.

I’ve been the bus driver.

I’ve been the person opening the building before the sun came up and the person making sure everything was ready for the next morning.

I’ve been the school leader.

And today, I’m the owner.

There is something powerful about having experienced nearly every position within an industry before being responsible for leading an organization within it.

You see things differently.

You understand the exhaustion behind a teacher’s smile. You understand the pressure of managing classrooms, comforting families, maintaining compliance, and ensuring that every child is safe, loved, and learning.

You also understand that the people doing this work are human.

And somewhere along my journey, I began asking myself a question that I believe our entire industry needs to confront:

Who protects the people who spend their lives protecting our children?

The Side of Childcare Nobody Talks About

When people think about childcare ownership, they often imagine someone sitting behind a desk, collecting tuition, making decisions, and overseeing employees.

But that picture leaves out so much of the reality.

Childcare ownership is not simply running a business. It is carrying the responsibility of an entire community.

It’s knowing that families are trusting you with the people they love most.

It’s knowing that teachers depend on your business to support their own families.

It’s understanding how much weight rests on every decision, because enrollment, staffing, finances, and a reputation built over years all depend on one another.

And sometimes, it’s having to carry all of that while the public sees only a fraction of what is happening behind the scenes.

I’ve learned that being the owner doesn’t mean you stop being the teacher, the cook, the driver, or the person willing to sweep the floors.

Sometimes, it means you’re still all of those people, just with significantly more responsibility.

Accountability Should Never Be Optional

Let me be very clear about something.

I believe in accountability.

I believe in licensing standards.

I believe in inspections, documentation, appropriate supervision, and the responsibility every childcare program has to protect children.

Children deserve nothing less.

But I also believe that accountability should be fair, consistent, and grounded in facts.

And I believe we need to have an honest conversation about what happens when the systems designed to protect children can be pulled into personal conflicts, misunderstandings, or disputes between families and schools.

Every concern deserves to be taken seriously.

Every allegation deserves an appropriate review.

But an allegation and a substantiated finding are not the same thing.

And in an age where information travels faster than facts, the distinction matters.

Protecting children and protecting due process should never be competing priorities.

We should be able to do both.

Ownership Carries the Weight

One of the most difficult lessons I’ve learned as a business owner is that you can build a strong organization, hire experienced leaders, establish clear expectations, and still face seasons that test everything you built.

As an owner, you are ultimately accountable for the organization.

I accept that responsibility.

But accountability at the ownership level should not eliminate accountability at every other level.

Strong schools need directors who lead, administrators who follow through, teachers who are supported and held to professional standards, and owners who create systems that make all of those expectations clear.

When something goes wrong, the public often looks for one person to blame.

Usually, that person is the owner.

But real organizational improvement requires us to look deeper than a name on a business license.

We have to examine communication, training, documentation, supervision, leadership decisions, and the systems surrounding the people involved.

That’s how we build better schools.

Not by assigning blame as quickly as possible, but by understanding what actually happened and making meaningful changes.

The People Behind the Classrooms Matter, Too

Early childhood educators are among the most important professionals in our communities.

Yet we continue to operate in an industry where burnout, staffing shortages, emotional exhaustion, and financial pressure are treated as normal.

They shouldn’t be.

I’ve worked alongside extraordinary educators.

I’ve watched teachers give more than their job descriptions required. I’ve seen people show up for children during difficult moments in their own lives.

I’ve seen the beauty of what happens when a team truly believes in the mission of a school.

And I’ve also seen what happens when those same people feel unsupported, misunderstood, or afraid that one difficult interaction could threaten their careers.

We cannot continue asking educators to give everything while ignoring the systems surrounding them.

And we cannot expect childcare owners to build sustainable, high-quality programs without acknowledging the enormous responsibilities they carry.

A healthy childcare industry requires healthy organizations, supported educators, accountable leadership, and informed families.

Every piece matters.

I Didn’t Build My Career From Behind a Desk

I think this is the part of my story that matters most.

Before I ever became a CEO, I knew what it meant to serve.

I knew what it meant to prepare meals, transport children, manage classrooms, support families, and work through the everyday challenges of early childhood education.

Those experiences shaped the leader I am today.

They taught me humility.

They taught me resilience.

They taught me that leadership isn’t about having the highest title in the building.

It’s about understanding the people whose work makes the building function.

And they taught me that sometimes, standing up for an industry means being willing to say things that may make people uncomfortable.

Not because you want conflict.

But because you want change.

It’s Time for a Different Conversation

I am not interested in creating an environment where childcare programs are above criticism.

Quite the opposite.

I want stronger schools.

I want better-trained leaders.

I want educators who feel valued.

I want families who feel heard.

I want licensing systems that protect children while maintaining fairness and transparency.

And I want childcare owners to have a meaningful voice in conversations about the regulations, expectations, and challenges that shape our industry.

We need conversations about the difference between accountability and public condemnation.

We need conversations about the responsibilities of school leadership.

We need conversations about educator retention, sustainable operations, and the emotional realities of owning a childcare business.

And we need to acknowledge that the people responsible for caring for children deserve dignity, fairness, and support, too.

This Is Just the Beginning

Over the coming weeks, I will be sharing more about my journey in early childhood education.

The lessons.

The victories.

The mistakes.

The difficult decisions.

And the realities that rarely make it into conversations about childcare ownership.

Some stories will celebrate the extraordinary people who helped me build my career.

Others will challenge the way we think about leadership, accountability, and the systems governing our schools.

I won’t pretend to have every answer.

But I do have experience, perspective, and a willingness to ask difficult questions.

Because after years of serving children, families, and educators, I’ve come to believe something deeply:

We cannot build a stronger future for children while overlooking the people responsible for creating it.

And that is a conversation worth having.

With purpose and conviction,

Shavonda Lawson
